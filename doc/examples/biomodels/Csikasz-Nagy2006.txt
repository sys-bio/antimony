Abstract: 
We propose a protein interaction network for the regulation of DNA synthesis
and mitosis that emphasizes the universality of the regulatory system among eukaryotic
cells. The idiosyncrasies of cell cycle regulation in particular organisms can be
attributed, we claim, to specific settings of rate constants in the dynamic network
of chemical reactions. The values of these rate constants are determined ultimately
by the genetic makeup of an organism. To support these claims, we convert the reaction
mechanism into a set of governing kinetic equations and provide parameter values
(specific to budding yeast, fission yeast, frog eggs, and mammalian cells) that account
for many curious features of cell cycle regulation in these organisms. Using one-parameter
bifurcation diagrams, we show how overall cell growth drives progression through
the cell cycle, how cell-size homeostasis can be achieved by two different strategies,
and how mutations remodel bifurcation diagrams and create unusual cell-division phenotypes.
The relation between gene dosage and phenotype can be summarized compactly in two-parameter
bifurcation diagrams. Our approach provides a theoretical framework in which to understand
both the universality and particularity of cell cycle regulation, and to construct,
in modular fashion, increasingly complex models of the networks controlling cell
growth and division.
